We stopped designing for project management creative momentum

A work-management product for creative agencies was built, shipped — and quietly ignored. The problem was never the feature set. It was that the software asked designers, editors and creative directors to think like project managers before it would let them do their jobs.

Brief Founder-led B2B SaaS. Positioning was sharp; adoption was not.

A sentence the market understood, sitting on top of a product it didn’t

I knew Thaher before Draftroom. One day he walked me through the B2B SaaS product he was building for marketing agencies, and the pitch was a single line:

Manage your marketing agency in one place. Never switch to Sheets and Drive again.

Draftroom’s positioning

The line was right. The product underneath it wasn’t — and Thaher knew it. That was the whole brief he gave me: the statement is solid, but the solution isn’t.

The obvious read was that Draftroom needed to become a better Trello. Cleaner boards, faster cards, tighter Slack-style comments. Three weeks of onboarding sessions said something else entirely.

Audit Five surfaces, none of them talking to each other.

A system that had never actually existed

Creative teams run on briefs, feedback rounds and deliverables. Every time work moved between those stages, the process broke — not slowly, but completely.

The real workflow was scattered across WhatsApp threads, Drive folders, email chains, spreadsheets and verbal agreements made in passing. Nothing was connected to anything else.

Failure 01

Designers worked blind

  • No reliable way to find the latest brief or the correct file version without asking a person
  • Getting feedback meant chasing people across three different platforms
  • No visibility into where a deliverable actually sat in the review process
Failure 02

Humans were the integration layer

  • Strategists and editors spent their day fielding status requests instead of doing the work
  • Every miscommunication needed a person to resolve it manually
  • A shared system should have absorbed all of this automatically. There wasn’t one
Failure 03

Risk compounded quietly

  • WhatsApp and Drive were never built for production tracking
  • Work reached clients having passed through review stages without formal sign-off
  • Every skipped approval was an unreviewed deliverable, and the count only went up

The cost was real and it was daily. It just never appeared on anyone’s dashboard, because there was no dashboard — and no system to put one on.

Insight Embedded sessions with designers, strategists, editors and creative directors.

The humans behind the screens

In product work we reduce people to “users” very quickly. But the people on the other side of Draftroom were not a segment — they were individuals with habits, frustrations and strongly held mental models about how creative work gets made.

So before designing anything, I sat with them: designers, strategists, editors and creative directors, each of whom had lived through the broken workflow firsthand. I wasn’t collecting feature requests. I wanted to see how they actually think, talk, decide and hand off.

The product wasn’t motivating anyone to track their work — because it had been designed for technical teams and handed to creative ones.

Key finding

Creative professionals don’t naturally think in rigid workflows, statuses or structured task management. That isn’t a training problem to be solved with a better onboarding tour. It is how the work is shaped.

For Draftroom to be adopted, it had to stop feeling like project management software and start feeling like part of the creative process. Products aren’t adopted because they have better features. They’re adopted when they fit the way people already work.

Four opportunities came out of the research

01Streamline task assignment

Eliminate the manual chasing. No creator should have to ask what they are supposed to be working on next.

02Minimise tool switching

Bring every disjointed conversation into one interface, instead of bouncing between four to track a single deliverable.

03Surface status upfront

Show where work stands before it becomes a problem. A missed review discovered after delivery is expensive rework.

04Build guidance into context

Put the brief, the assets and the feedback exactly where the work happens. Nobody should leave the tool to do their job.

Reframe The decision everything else follows from.

Designing for momentum, not completion

The product was not being used because it was asking creative people to think like project managers. That was never going to work — not with better onboarding, not with a cleaner board, not with more features.

So we changed what the product was optimising for. We stopped designing for task completion and started designing for creative momentum.

Two decisions carried almost all of the weight, and neither of them was a feature.

Revision 01 Vocabulary rewrite. Shipped before any interface change.

The first mega fix

Rewrite the language before touching the interface

One thing became unmissable in research: the product was speaking the language of project management, while its users spoke the language of creative work.

Task. Project. Status. Assignee. Mechanical words, handed to people who think in campaigns, concepts, revisions, approvals and deliverables.

Draftroom wasn’t difficult to use. It was difficult to relate to. So the first major design decision wasn’t adding a feature or redesigning a dashboard — it was rewriting the product’s entire vocabulary to match the words users already had.

Design principle

People shouldn’t have to learn a new language to use a product. The product should adapt to the language they already have.

Adoption didn’t improve because we added capability. It improved because the product stopped feeling foreign. When software speaks your language, it doesn’t register as new software — it registers as familiar.

Revision 02 Red retired from work states. Reserved for notifications only.

The second mega fix

Design for calm, not pressure

Most project management tools run on manufactured urgency. High-priority labels. Overdue badges. Red alerts. Constant reminders.

That approach works for operations teams. It does not work for creative ones. Creativity doesn’t thrive under sustained pressure — it thrives when people feel clear, focused and in control of what’s in front of them.

So instead of designing around deadlines, we designed around readiness.

BeforeHigh priority Ranks the work by how loudly it can shout, and says nothing about what to do.
→
AfterNeeds you Names the one person who can move it forward, right now.
BeforePending A dead state. True of everything, actionable for no one.
→
AfterYour turn A handoff. The work has arrived and it is now yours.

Red was pulled out of work states entirely and reserved for notifications alone. Nothing a designer owns is ever coloured like an error.

The interface stopped demanding attention and started directing it. The goal was never to make work feel urgent — it was to make it feel approachable.

This wasn’t about removing deadlines or reducing accountability. It was about changing the emotional experience of work.

When the interface feels calm instead of stressful, designers spend less energy reacting to the tool and more energy making the thing.

Final Four opportunities, one product.

What shipped

Every decision below traces back to one of the four opportunities, and all of them to the reframe: momentum over completion.

01

A single place for all creative work

Every brief, cycle, deliverable and feedback round lives in one interface. No WhatsApp, no Drive folders, no spreadsheet running in parallel. The work and the record of the work became the same object — which is the only version of this that survives contact with a deadline.

02

Cards that say why, not when

Every card shows the creator what needs their attention and why. Not a due date. Not a status label. A reason line: who is waiting, what they said, and what has to happen before the work can move forward.

03

Status that matches how creative work actually moves

Script. Draft. Final. Feedback round. Approved. The language of the product matches the language of the work, so creators never translate their own reality into software vocabulary before they can act on it.

04

Visibility without surveillance

Leads and managers see where every piece of work stands without sending a single message. Not a dashboard full of metrics nobody reads — a calm, specific signal about what is moving and what is stuck.

05

Built for the field as much as the studio

An architect on a construction site and a designer at their desk are the same person in two contexts. Draftroom serves both: a full planning surface on desktop, and a five-action field tool on mobile — offline-resilient, and operable with one hand.

Delivered Shipped before the informal infrastructure collapsed.

Outcome

Draftroom launched before creative teams hit a breaking point. The informal infrastructure — WhatsApp groups, Drive folders, verbal agreements — was replaced by a single connected workflow rather than being left to fail first.

Tool consolidation 5 → 1

Communication and production tracking moved from five fragmented surfaces onto one product.

Process compliance 100%

Work moving through formal review stages went from ad hoc to fully structured.

Speed to delivery 3 weeks

Core desktop and mobile surfaces researched, designed, tested and shipped on schedule.

Next round The framework became the platform.

What it turned into

Beyond resolving the day-to-day friction of creative production, the My Day framework and the field-first mobile patterns were adopted as the foundation for how the product scales. A UX framework built for one designer’s working day is now the basis for team-level workflows, client visibility layers and multi-brand production environments.

In later iterations we designed and launched voice capture, a field inbox and AI-generated reason lines — giving field users a way to document an observation in under thirty seconds, and feeding that context straight back into the desktop review surface.

Sign-off

Not another task manager — a workspace that respects the creative process.

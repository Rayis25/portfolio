We stopped designing for project management and started designing for creative momentum.

A work-management product for creative agencies was built, shipped — and quietly ignored. The problem was never the feature set. It was that the software asked designers, editors and creative directors to think like project managers before it would let them do their jobs.

About

Draftroom is a B2B SaaS product that runs a marketing agency in one place, so teams never fall back to Sheets and Drive.

I joined as Lead UX Designer with a sharp brief from the founder: the positioning was right, the product underneath it wasn’t. Three weeks later we had shipped a rewrite of how the product speaks, thinks and paces itself — and creative teams started using it.

Problem

A system that had never actually existed

Creative teams run on briefs, feedback rounds and deliverables. Every time work moved between those stages, the process broke — not slowly, but completely.

The real workflow was scattered across WhatsApp threads, Drive folders, email chains, spreadsheets and verbal agreements made in passing. Nothing was connected to anything else.

Failure 01

Designers worked blind

  • No reliable way to find the latest brief or correct file version without asking a person
  • Getting feedback meant chasing people across three different platforms
  • No visibility into where a deliverable sat in the review process
Failure 02

Humans were the integration layer

  • Strategists and editors spent their day fielding status requests instead of working
  • Every miscommunication needed a person to resolve it manually
  • A shared system should have absorbed all of this. There wasn’t one
Failure 03

Risk compounded quietly

  • WhatsApp and Drive were never built for production tracking
  • Work reached clients having passed review stages without formal sign-off
  • Every skipped approval was an unreviewed deliverable, and the count only went up
Research

The humans behind the screens

In product work we reduce people to “users” very quickly. But the people on the other side of Draftroom were individuals with habits, frustrations and strongly held mental models about how creative work gets made.

So before designing anything, I sat with them: designers, strategists, editors and creative directors, each of whom had lived through the broken workflow firsthand. I wasn’t collecting feature requests. I wanted to see how they actually think, talk, decide and hand off.

The product wasn’t motivating anyone to track their work — because it had been designed for technical teams and handed to creative ones.

Key finding

Creative professionals don’t naturally think in rigid workflows, statuses or structured task management. That isn’t a training problem to be solved with a better onboarding tour. It is how the work is shaped.

Products aren’t adopted because they have better features. They’re adopted when they fit the way people already work.

Approach

Designing for momentum, not completion

The product was not being used because it was asking creative people to think like project managers. That was never going to work — not with better onboarding, not with a cleaner board, not with more features. Two decisions carried almost all of the weight, and neither of them was a feature.

Decision 01 — Language

Rewrite the language before touching the interface

The product was speaking the language of project management, while its users spoke the language of creative work. Task. Project. Status. Assignee. Mechanical words, handed to people who think in campaigns, concepts, revisions, approvals and deliverables.

Draftroom wasn’t difficult to use. It was difficult to relate to. So the first major design decision wasn’t adding a feature or redesigning a dashboard — it was rewriting the product’s entire vocabulary.

People shouldn’t have to learn a new language to use a product. The product should adapt to the language they already have.

Design principle

Adoption didn’t improve because we added capability. It improved because the product stopped feeling foreign. When software speaks your language, it doesn’t register as new software — it registers as familiar.

Decision 02 — Tone

Design for calm, not pressure

Most project management tools run on manufactured urgency. High-priority labels. Overdue badges. Red alerts. Constant reminders. That works for operations teams. It does not work for creative ones — creativity thrives when people feel clear, focused and in control.

So instead of designing around deadlines, we designed around readiness.

Before High priority Ranks the work by how loudly it can shout, and says nothing about what to do.
→
After Needs you Names the one person who can move it forward, right now.
Before Pending A dead state. True of everything, actionable for no one.
→
After Your turn A handoff. The work has arrived and it is now yours.

Red was pulled out of work states entirely and reserved for notifications alone. Nothing a designer owns is ever coloured like an error. The interface stopped demanding attention and started directing it — not to remove deadlines or reduce accountability, but to change the emotional experience of work.

An architect on a construction site and a designer at their desk are the same person in two contexts. A full planning surface on desktop, and a five-action field tool on mobile — offline-resilient, operable with one hand.

Next

What it turned into

The My Day framework and the field-first mobile patterns were adopted as the foundation for how the product scales. A UX framework built for one designer’s working day is now the basis for team-level workflows, client visibility layers and multi-brand production environments.

In later iterations we designed and launched voice capture, a field inbox and AI-generated reason lines — giving field users a way to document an observation in under thirty seconds, and feeding that context straight back into the desktop review surface.

Not another task manager — a workspace that respects the creative process.
